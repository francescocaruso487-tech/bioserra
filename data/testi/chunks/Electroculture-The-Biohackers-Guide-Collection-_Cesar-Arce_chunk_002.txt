 Fleming’s Right-Hand Rule and Underground Currents
The physics of coil orientation is not arbitrary—it is grounded in classical
electromagnetism.

[PAG 57]

conductor, it generates a circular magnetic field around it. If you point your
right-hand thumb in the direction of the current (i), the curl of your fingers
shows the orientation of the magnetic field lines (B).
This is why the winding direction of a spiral matters: clockwise or
counterclockwise defines how the induced field will align with Earth’s own.
A coil wound in harmony with the hemisphere strengthens resonance; a coil
wound against it weakens the effect.
Nature itself provides examples. Flowing underground water—especially
when enriched with salts and minerals—acts like a natural conductor. Its
movement generates weak but measurable electromagnetic fields, shaped
by flow speed, mineral content, and geological layers. Just as an electrical
wire produces a magnetic field, so do subterranean streams beneath our
feet.

 Mini-Experiment: Can a Small “Electroculture” Antenna Pick Up
Atmospheric Voltage?
Purpose
To test whether a tiny copper spring antenna with a pointed tip can
accumulate a measurable electric potential from the atmosphere in fair-
weather conditions, and to observe how this stored charge behaves like a
capacitor—showing an initial voltage that gradually discharges over time.
Date / Conditions
21 Sept 2025, sunny, ~21 °C, light wind, lawn soil slightly moist (typical
garden conditions).
Materials
Handheld digital multimeter (DC volts, input impedance ≈10
MΩ).
Two test leads (red to VΩmA, black to COM).
Copper spring antenna, height ≈4 cm, with a sharpened tip.
Temporary ground contact (probe pressed into soil/grass).
Location: Montreal, Canada (fair-weather day).
Setup
Red lead clipped to the copper spring (the “antenna”).
Black lead either (a) left unconnected (floating) or (b) pressed
into the ground/soil as a reference (“earth”).

[PAG 65]
Antenna placed upright on the grass with its pointed tip oriented
roughly toward Magnetic South.
Note: In Montreal (Northern Hemisphere) azimuthal orientation matters
less than the vertical geometry; the point simply concentrates the
local field at the tip.
Procedure & Results
1. Open reference (black lead not connected):
The meter showed ~0.02 V.
Interpretation: With no reference, the meter “floats” and
displays only tiny stray charges.
2. Earth-referenced (black lead in soil):
The meter fluctuated between ~3.4 and 3.8 V DC.
Interpretation: With ground as a reference, the antenna sits in
the fair-weather atmospheric electric field (typically ~100–
150 V/m downward). A 4 cm (0.04 m) vertical conductor in a
~100 V/m field should see about:

[PAG 66]
Your measured 3.4–3.8 V matches this expectation very closely. The
pointed tip further concentrates the charge locally, helping the meter
register the potential. Small fluctuations are normal (breeze, humidity,
contact pressure, and nearby body/lead movement).
What This Shows (and What It Doesn’t)
Shown: A tiny vertical conductor acquires a measurable DC
potential relative to earth due to the natural atmospheric
potential gradient, acting as a capacitor.
Not shown: Useful power. The available current is extremely
small (nanoamps–microamps). This is a voltage source with
very high internal resistance, not a practical power supply.
Controls & Follow-Up Tests (to strengthen the demonstration)
1. Height vs. voltage (key validation):
Use copper rods/springs of different heights (2 cm, 4 cm, 8 cm, 20
cm). Plot V vs. height. Expect an approximately linear trend (fair-
weather days), e.g., doubling height ≈ doubles voltage.

[PAG 67]
2. Load test (shows source resistance):
Insert a resistor between antenna and meter (e.g., 1 MΩ, 100 kΩ).
Watch the voltage collapse as the load increases. This demonstrates
low available current.
3. Material control (rule out galvanic effects):
Ensure both antenna and ground contact are the same metal
(copper) to minimize soil-battery/galvanic voltages. Moist soil +
dissimilar metals can create extra millivolts.
4. Shielding (zero check):
Put the antenna entirely inside a metal can tied to ground (a simple
Faraday shield). The reading should drop toward 0 V, confirming
you were measuring the external field.
5. Weather dependence:
Repeat on a humid or overcast day (the fair-weather field often
weakens) and before/after a storm front (field can change). Record
conditions.
6. Polarity swap:
Reverse the leads. The sign should invert (confirming a real
potential relative to earth).
7. Distance from you & cables:
Step back and keep leads still. Your body can act as a capacitor and
slightly modulate readings.
Safety Notes

[PAG 68]
Use short antennas in open areas only. Do not deploy tall rods
or work during storms; elevated conductors can attract
lightning.
Keep this purely as a sensing demo; don’t attempt to “harvest”
power from live mains or fences.

 How to Build Your First Antenna (Step by Step)
You don’t need a laboratory or a fortune to begin. The simplest
electroculture antenna can be built in under an hour with basic materials:
Materials:

[PAG 81]
1 wooden or bamboo pole (30–100 cm for pots, 1–2 m for
gardens).
1 roll of copper wire (1–2 mm thick).
A hammer or stone to secure the pole in soil.
Steps:
1. Prepare the pole. Insert it firmly into the soil, at least 10–20
cm deep. For pots, choose a smaller stick; for farms, taller poles
spaced every 10–20 m.
2. Anchor the wire. Twist the copper wire around the base of the
pole and insert the free end 5–10 cm into the soil—this is your
ground connection.
3. Spiral upwards. Wrap the wire in a clockwise direction ⟳
(Northern Hemisphere) or counterclockwise ⟲ (Southern
Hemisphere), leaving 2–3 cm between each turn.
4. Shape the tip. At the top, you may leave the wire loose, form a
spiral, or add a small crystal or decorative element.
5. Observe. Within days or weeks, many gardeners report stronger
seedlings, greener leaves, and fewer pests.

 Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
1. Wrong coil direction: Remember—clockwise in the North,
counterclockwise in the South. Reversing the direction may
neutralize or weaken the effect.
2. Poor grounding: If the bottom of the wire is not in contact with
moist soil, the circuit is incomplete. Always bury a portion of
the wire.

3. Dry soil: Electroculture amplifies conductivity; without water,
soil becomes an insulator. Keep the soil hydrated.
4. Overcrowding antennas: More is not always better. Space
them properly; overlapping fields may create confusion instead
of coherence.
5. Impatience: Nature works in rhythms. Some results—like
germination—can appear in days, but others, like soil vitality or
pest resistance, may take a season.
